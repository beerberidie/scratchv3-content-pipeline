Covid Brain: New Study Reveals How the Pandemic Accelerated Brain Ageing Across All Ages and Backgrounds
The pandemic’s long-term impact on brain health has been revealed by a groundbreaking study, which suggests that the experience of lockdowns and social isolation may have accelerated brain ageing for people across all demographics. The research, published in Nature, found that the average biological age of participants rose by nearly six months following the pandemic, regardless of whether they contracted Covid-19. This discovery challenges previous assumptions that cognitive decline is solely linked to illness or aging and highlights the profound effects of prolonged stress and social disconnection on mental health.
The Study: A Deeper Look into Brain Ageing
The research utilised brain scans from over 15,000 healthy individuals to develop models that predict biological age based on structural and functional brain characteristics. These models were then applied to nearly 1,000 participants in the UK Biobank study, a long-term project tracking the health of middle-aged and older adults. Half of the participants had undergone brain scans before the pandemic, while the rest had them during or after the lockdowns. The results showed that the pandemic had "significantly" accelerated brain ageing, with an average deviation of 5.5 months in biological age compared to expected age.
This finding is particularly concerning because a faster rate of brain ageing could increase the risk of cognitive decline and mental health issues later in life. The study’s lead author, Dr. Ali-Reza Mohammadi-Nejad, noted that the pandemic’s impact was not limited to those who fell ill but extended to everyone, regardless of their exposure to the virus. “It really shows how much the experience of the pandemic itself - everything from isolation to uncertainty - may have affected our brain health,” he said.
Factors Driving Accelerated Brain Ageing
The researchers identified several factors that likely contributed to the observed effects. Prolonged periods of social isolation, disrupted routines, and heightened anxiety were linked to increased stress levels, which can impair cognitive functions such as memory, emotional regulation, and decision-making. The study also found that men, older individuals, and those from deprived backgrounds were more vulnerable to these changes.
Dr. Joanna Konstantopoulou, a health psychologist, explained that chronic social disconnection and sustained stress can "impair memory, emotional regulation, and executive functioning." She highlighted studies showing that women may have fared better due to physiological differences, such as the role of oestrogen in regulating the HPA axis - a system involved in responding to stress. “Oestrogen appears to buffer some of the negative effects of prolonged stress,” she said.
The Broader Implications for Brain Health
The findings underscore that brain health is not solely determined by illness but also by how we live, connect, and cope with pressure. The pandemic has exposed the fragility of mental health in the face of unprecedented uncertainty, raising questions about how to mitigate these effects moving forward.
Professor Dorothee Auer, a neuroimaging expert involved in the study, acknowledged that reversing the observed changes remains uncertain but expressed cautious optimism. “We can’t test whether the changes we saw will reverse, but it’s certainly possible, and that’s an encouraging thought,” she said. This suggests that interventions such as social engagement, mental health support, and stress-reduction techniques could help counteract the pandemic’s impact on brain ageing.
A Call for Action: Supporting Brain Health in a Post-Pandemic World
The study serves as a reminder that the long-term effects of the pandemic extend beyond physical health and into the realm of cognitive well-being. As society rebuilds, it is crucial to prioritise mental health initiatives that address the psychological toll of lockdowns and isolation. This includes promoting social connections, encouraging physical activity, and fostering environments that reduce chronic stress.
For individuals, the findings offer a call to action: maintaining healthy habits, staying socially connected, and seeking support when needed can help protect brain health. As Dr. Mohammadi-Nejad noted, the pandemic has left an indelible mark on our minds, but it also highlights the resilience of the human spirit and the potential for recovery.
In a world where mental health is increasingly recognised as vital to overall well-being, the study on "Covid Brain" serves as a powerful reminder that our brains are shaped not just by what happens to us, but by how we navigate life’s challenges. The pandemic has revealed a new reality: that brain health is as much about connection and coping as it is about illness.

The study also highlighted that brain ageing was particularly accelerated in men, older individuals, and those from deprived backgrounds. Moving Forward
The study underscores the importance of addressing the long-term cognitive effects of the pandemic. Further research is needed to explore potential interventions and strategies to support brain health and mitigate the accelerated ageing observed during this period. By understanding the factors that contribute to brain ageing, we can take proactive steps to protect our cognitive well-being and promote healthy ageing for all.
